Machinarium - 88/100

Review date: September 25, 2026, Author: Swiper4ik

One of the Best Adaptations of a Robotic World.
Machinarium stands as the absolute pinnacle of Amanita Design's work. It presents a genuinely wonderful world — one defined by rust, scrap metal, and chaos, yet filled with incredible warmth and charm. Finding another project with this level of hand-drawn detail, where every speck of rust feels intentional, is remarkably rare. The 2D perspective is far from a limitation; in fact, every single screen looks like a painting crafted specifically for an art gallery.

Beyond its striking visual presentation, Machinarium offers a touching narrative and engaging puzzle gameplay. While certain riddles can be quite challenging, the game never feels unfair. The music, however, is on a whole different level. Discovering such a gorgeous, emotionally resonant original soundtrack in a small indie title is a wonderful surprise. The track The Sea, featured in the second area, deserves a special mention — it is a breathtaking symphony that conveys the deep, unspoken lives of these mechanical inhabitants without a single line of dialogue. The only real drawbacks are a few overly obtuse puzzle steps and the game's relatively short length.

The story begins with Josef, a small robot we must initially reassemble piece by piece. Despite starting in a scrapyard, Josef isn't a blank slate — he possesses a distinct personality, memories, and a clear history. The game cleverly avoids hand-holding; there are no intrusive markers, and you naturally progress toward the city simply because the world organically guides you there. As the narrative unfolds, we learn that Josef was victimized by the Black Cap Brotherhood — a group of local bullies who are now planning to bomb the city. While the bomb plot feels slightly cartoonish, the game handles themes of bullying and social inequality with surprising care. Thought bubbles showing short memories remind us that even in a world devoid of humans, sentient beings face familiar societal issues. Eventually, Josef rescues his childhood friend Berta, stops the villains, and together they fly away into the horizon.

From a gameplay perspective, the level design is exceptional. The puzzles are varied and deeply tied to the mechanical nature of the world. Movement involves adjusting Josef's height, scavenging for items, and combining objects in inventive ways. The city feels interconnected, and its inhabitants feel like a real community. To progress, you must examine every corner carefully.

Unfortunately, the hint system can be tedious. Accessing the in-game walkthrough book requires completing a slow mini-game every single time, and the book's state resets whenever you re-enter an area. Additionally, the mobile port suffers slightly, as precise tap-controls lack the accuracy of a desktop cursor.

Verdict:

A brilliant 2D puzzle adventure that offers a fair challenge without high-stress hazard mechanics or damage-dealing enemies. It invites players to find beauty in scrap metal, solve clever environmental riddles, and experience a charming story from start to finish. Machinarium is an easy recommendation for almost anyone, with its few flaws limited to minor UI inconveniences and a brief runtime.
