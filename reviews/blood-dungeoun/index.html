Blood Dungeoun - 71/100

A stylish roguelike that restores faith in the genre.
In truth, Blood Dungeon is a unique roguelike featuring excellent progression and run variety. Surprisingly, your first few attempts might feel difficult and sluggish as you try to figure out how to navigate and progress. However, once you spend a bit more time with it, the mechanics click, and you begin to see a tangible improvement in your skills. You adapt to the game's physics, push further through the levels, and feel a natural drive to survive longer in every single run.

The smooth pixel art—though simple and foundational—is so pleasing to the eye that you won't notice any rough edges. The game is welcoming to newcomers; as soon as a player starts acting with intention, they cease to be a novice. This title offers a fantastic entry point for those who usually avoid roguelikes. Unlike Balatro, which immediately disadvantages players who want to chase high scores without spending hours studying complex mathematical formulas and synergies, Blood Dungeon delivers a fresh and distinct experience.

It’s a true haven for fans of roguelikes, pixel art, and Dungeon DnB soundtracks. The lack of a explicit story doesn't prevent you from immersing yourself in its world and characters. You are simply given locations, playable heroes with distinct stats, and the choice to jump straight into combat.

First, it’s worth spending a moment to get comfortable with the hub and controls. The core loop is straightforward: there is a central hub and an initial area where the primary gameplay takes place. Defeating a boss grants access to new worlds. Thus, gameplay is split into several maps, each with unique enemies and environmental features.

What immediately stands out—and is executed exceptionally well—is the fixed, vertical map layout. Movement primarily happens vertically, which feels like a remarkably cool design choice. While vertical navigation isn't entirely new to gaming, compared to mainstream roguelikes, it offers a completely different pacing. The movement mechanics are equally satisfying: players don't just run, but jump and climb across tiles. The wall-clinging mechanic is both engaging and essential, as you'll be relying on it constantly.

Verdict:

A relatively straightforward yet thoroughly enjoyable title. It doesn't overpromise deep, complex mechanics, but it delivers on what it sets out to do. Blood Dungeon comes highly recommended for players who haven't clicked with the roguelike genre before but are willing to give it one last shot.

Combat is tightly tied to leveling up through experience. Enemy waves evolve over time, increasing in density and strength. To stay ahead, you must level up by collecting blood dropped by fallen foes. Reaching a new level pauses the game and opens an upgrade selection menu. This is where build variety shines: you can pick up secondary weapons, increase your character's size, boost luck, upgrade equipment, gain movement speed, jump higher, or leave a trail of fire in your wake. These options allow for wildly different tactics across sessions. Beyond experience, you also earn coins during runs to buy items directly on the field.

The hub area is also worth spending time in. Here, you can switch characters—each coming with different health pools, movement speeds, and starting weapons—or visit the shop to purchase permanent character upgrades using bones. Bones are awarded at the end of a run: the better your performance and milestones, the more bones you bring back.

However, the game is not without its flaws. Bosses typically spawn around player levels 9–10, constantly harassing you while taking a significant amount of time to defeat. While a challenging boss fight serves as a great milestone, beating it causes a jarring drop in momentum. Wave spawns slow down, experience gains stall, blood drops feel insufficient, and enemy health pools bloat. This is the game's main flaw: one would expect the late game to offer richer, faster-paced content, but instead, progression grinds to a halt. Continuing past this point remains possible, but it shifts from fun to a tedious hardcore grind.
