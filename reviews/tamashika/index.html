Tamashika - 53/100

Review date: August 25, 2026, Author: Swiper4ik

Tamashika is a short, unforgiving game where you barely feel like you're actually playing.
The main campaign consists of just 8 core levels designed to drag you through all five stages of grief. Once completed, the game leaves you with a choice: call it quits or continue torturing yourself across 300 randomized procedural stages. While the rich atmosphere and dense visual design instantly grab your attention, the core gameplay loop feels hollow and deeply frustrating.

It presents itself as difficult, but compared to genuine hardcore "troll" platformers, Tamashika isn't truly challenging — it’s just irritating. It offers no room for casual players to catch their breath, nor does it give veterans a satisfying mechanical test. Due to punishingly strict timing and precise input requirements, you stop feeling like you're playing a game and instead find yourself executing mindless routine against a gorgeous backdrop.

It’s hard to tell what the developers were aiming for. They seem to troll casual gamers with art-house aesthetics and short runtimes, while baffling hardcore players with artificial difficulty instead of memorable mechanics. Dedicated masochists might find some joy here, but the average gamer will only be left confused.

Yeah… Masochism. That’s truly the single word to describe this game.

Right from the start, you're greeted by a tutorial filled with Japanese characters. The choice of Japanese feels completely out of place: while the game does support other languages, it constantly shoves oversized Japanese text onto your screen during gameplay. It doesn't look stylish at all. It feels like a forced attempt to pass itself off as a "niche" art-house title, which it clearly isn't.

After that, without any cutscenes or narrative introduction, you're abruptly thrown into learning how to run and kill little "green men." And that’s where the "fun" begins. You are given two weapon types: melee (a knife) and ranged (a pistol). The melee combat forces you into punishingly strict timing windows, and it’s executed horribly. The window to execute a knife kill lasts a second at most, but in practice, it feels like mere milliseconds. You could go through the entire game without ever understanding what you're doing wrong, ultimately relying on pure luck that the green guy just happens to die.

Standard shooting, on the other hand, feels effortless: just aim and pull the trigger once. But nobody is going to let you just shoot freely — the game chokes you with strict timing again. These green enemies don't just slash at you; they hide across the map, execute multi-hit knife combos, and shoot back. In those moments, you have to parry every single movement, followed by a tiny window where you might get to parry again before finally executing a finisher.

This level of difficulty isn't rewarding; it's just irritating. As you force yourself to adapt and mechanically repeat these actions, you stop feeling anything at all. You lose the sensation of playing a finished, cohesive product, feeling instead like you're grinding through a series of aimless challenge rooms with no real purpose.

Verdict:

Tamashika is a project that looks modern and reasonably stylish. It operates strictly on its own terms, yet fails to deliver unique mechanics, satisfying feedback, or any genuine sense of enjoyment.

It is far too oppressive for average players due to artificially strict timing, yet completely uninteresting for hardcore enthusiasts due to its shallow underlying design. Tamashika serves best as a background game while listening to a podcast, or as a last resort if you somehow stumble upon it during a content drought.

After clearing the initial 8 levels, you won't encounter any more handcrafted content. From that point on, all the project has to offer is a set of dynamic, infinitely generated modes.

Everything that follows is reduced to a series of randomized, endless stages with no final destination. It’s a baffling design choice: mindless procedural generation alone does nothing to make the core gameplay loop any more engaging.
